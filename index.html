Money / Geld Symbol: \(M\) Any commodity or asset generally accepted by the public as payment for goods and services or in the legal discharge of debt and financial obligations. Medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value. Mohr §14.1
Fiduciary (Fiat) Money 💡 Symbol: Paper & Coin Currency that possesses zero intrinsic commercial value (paper notes, base coins, bank ledger bits) whose value derives exclusively from public trust and state legal tender status. Intrinsically worthless, public trust, legal tender. Mohr §14.1
Narrow Money (M1) Symbol: \(M_1\) The primary transactional money aggregate comprising notes and coins in active circulation outside the banking sector plus all check and transmission (demand) deposits of domestic private entities with commercial banks. Cash outside banks, demand deposits, immediate transaction liquidity. Mohr §14.1
Quasi-Money (Near Money) 💡 Symbol: \(QM\) Short-term and medium-term deposits (including ordinary savings accounts) held by the private sector with banking institutions that serve as a store of value and can be converted into transaction money rapidly at negligible cost. Short- & medium-term deposits, near-money liquidity, store of value. Mohr §14.1
Intermediate Money (M2) Symbol: \(M_2\) An intermediate monetary aggregate defined mathematically as narrow money supply plus quasi-money: \(M_2 = M_1 + \text{Quasi-Money}\) (short-term and medium-term commercial bank deposits). \(M_1\) + Quasi-Money, intermediate liquidity, savings deposits. Mohr §14.1
Broad Money (M3) Symbol: \(M_3\) The most comprehensive monetary aggregate recognized by the SARB, defined as \(M_2\) plus all long-term deposits with commercial banks; serves as the primary macro-prudential monetary indicator. \(M_2\) + Long-term deposits, broadest aggregate, SARB anchor indicator. Mohr §14.1
Cash Reserve Requirement Symbol: \(r\) The legally mandated statutory minimum fraction of total bank deposits that private commercial banks must deposit in non-interest-bearing accounts with the South African Reserve Bank. Statutory minimum percentage, non-interest-bearing SARB vault reserve, credit governor. Mohr §14.2
Repurchase (Repo) Rate 💡 Symbol: \(i_{\text{repo}}\) The benchmark policy interest rate at which the SARB tenders liquidity to registered commercial banks at weekly repurchase auctions to cover their daily interbank liquidity shortage. SARB benchmark policy rate, weekly repo auctions, liquidity deficit refinancing. Mohr §14.4
Prime Overdraft Rate Symbol: \(i_{\text{prime}}\) The standard benchmark lending rate charged by private commercial banks to their most creditworthy corporate and retail borrowers, traditionally set in South Africa at 350 basis points above the repo rate (\(i_{\text{prime}} = i_{\text{repo}} + 3.50\%\)). Commercial bank benchmark lending rate, fixed spread over repo (+3.50%). Mohr §14.4
Active Cash Balances Symbol: \(L_1(Y)\) Money demanded by households and enterprises to bridge the temporal gap between income receipt and expenditure (transactions motive) and for unforeseen contingencies (precautionary motive); strictly a positive function of real national income \(Y\). Transactions & precautionary motives, positive function of income \(Y\), interest-inelastic. Mohr §14.3
Speculative Balances 💡 Symbol: \(L_2(i)\) Idle cash balances held as financial assets to take advantage of future expected movements in bond prices and interest rates; inversely related to the nominal interest rate \(i\). Speculative asset demand, inverse function of interest rate \(i\), opportunity cost of cash. Mohr §14.3
Liquidity Trap 💡 Symbol: Horizontal \(M_d\) A Keynesian market boundary where interest rates fall so low that the speculative demand for money becomes perfectly elastic; additional central bank liquidity injections are hoarded into idle cash rather than lowering interest rates. Infinitely elastic speculative demand, horizontal \(M_d\) segment, monetary policy impotence. Mohr §14.3

Dedicated Stepper Cards

🏦 The Commercial Bank Credit Creation & Deposit Expansion Process

How private commercial banks expand the money supply through fractional reserve banking, and the real-world leakages that constrain the theoretical multiplier.

Step 1
Primary Cash Inflow to Initial Bank
An economic agent deposits a primary injection of \(\Delta R = \text{R}10\,000\) in new legal tender cash into Commercial Bank A, increasing bank assets (cash reserves) and liabilities (checkable deposit) equally.
Step 2
Statutory Cash Reserve Retention
Assuming a statutory reserve requirement of \(r = 10\%\) (\(0.10\)), Bank A is legally required to hold \(\text{R}1\,000\) in reserve with the SARB. This leaves \(\text{R}9\,000\) as excess loanable reserves.
Step 3
Credit Extension & New Deposit Creation
Bank A lends the \(\text{R}9\,000\) excess reserves to Borrower 1 by crediting a demand deposit. Bank A has literally created \(\text{R}9\,000\) in new book money out of thin air. Borrower 1 writes a check/EFT to pay a supplier.
Step 4
Secondary Interbank Expansion Rounds
The supplier deposits the \(\text{R}9\,000\) into Commercial Bank B. Bank B must retain \(10\%\) (\(\text{R}900\)) and lends out the remaining \(90\%\) (\(\text{R}8\,100\)) to Borrower 2, propagating across banks in a diminishing geometric sequence: \(\text{R}10\,000 + \text{R}9\,000 + \text{R}8\,100 + \text{R}7\,290 + \dots\)
Step 5
Theoretical Maximum Cumulative Money Creation
Summing the infinite convergent series produces the maximum total deposit expansion:
\(\Delta D = \Delta R \times \frac{1}{r} = \text{R}10\,000 \times \frac{1}{0.10} = \text{R}10\,000 \times 10 = \text{R}100\,000\)
Net newly created credit money equals \(\Delta D - \Delta R = \text{R}100\,000 - \text{R}10\,000 = \text{R}90\,000\).
Step 6
Real-World Constraints & Cash Drains
In practice, the multiplier is significantly smaller than \(\frac{1}{r}\) due to: (a) Cash drain (the public retains currency rather than re-depositing), (b) Excess reserves (risk-averse banks hold precautionary liquidity), and (c) Lack of creditworthy borrower demand.

📈 The SARB Repurchase (Repo) Rate Monetary Policy Transmission Mechanism

The chronological six-stage transmission mechanism through which central bank interest rate decisions propagate through the commercial financial system to manage aggregate demand and anchor inflation expectations.

Step 1
SARB MPC Policy Rate Decision
To counteract rising demand-pull or wage-push inflation expectations, the SARB Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) raises the benchmark Repurchase (Repo) Rate (\(i_{\text{repo}} \uparrow\)) at its weekly Wednesday liquidity refinancing auction.
Step 2
Interbank & Prime Overdraft Pass-Through
Commercial banks face an immediate increase in the cost of refinancing their daily liquidity deficit at the SARB window. Within 24 hours, commercial banks pass on this cost by lifting the benchmark Prime Overdraft Rate (\(i_{\text{prime}} \uparrow = i_{\text{repo}} + 3.50\%\)) and floating deposit rates.
Step 3
Credit Channel Tightening & Cost Escalation
Borrowing costs for households and corporations escalate across all credit agreements (mortgage bonds, vehicle asset finance, business overdrafts). Concurrently, the incentive to save rises as fixed-deposit yields increase.
Step 4
Contraction in Private Domestic Expenditure
Higher debt servicing costs erode household disposable income, suppressing autonomous consumption of durables (\(C \downarrow\)). Simultaneously, higher financing hurdle rates lead business firms to postpone capital expenditure (\(I \downarrow\)).
Step 5
Aggregate Demand Contraction
Reduced domestic absorption contracts total expenditure, shifting the Aggregate Demand curve downwards/leftwards (\(AD = C + I + G + X - Z \downarrow\)), eliminating the positive output gap.
Step 6
Disinflation & Price Stability
Firms face excess inventory and dampening demand, reducing their pricing power. Upward pressure on consumer prices dissipates, driving headline CPI inflation (\(\pi \downarrow\)) back toward the SARB 4.5% target midpoint.
Repo Rate \(i_{\text{repo}} \uparrow\) ➔ Prime Rate \(i_{\text{prime}} \uparrow\) ➔ Credit Cost \(\uparrow\) ➔ Private Spending \(C, I \downarrow\) ➔ Aggregate Demand \(AD \downarrow\) ➔ Inflation Rate \(\pi \downarrow\)

Publication-grade fiscal economics definitions distinguishing market failure modes, externality structures, equity criteria, and budgetary stances.

Economic Term & Symbol Official Exam Definition Key Keywords Emphasized Prescribed Citation
Market Failure Allocative Inefficiency An economic situation in which the free, unhindered price mechanism fails to allocate scarce resources socially efficiently, yielding sub-optimal output and net deadweight social welfare loss. Inefficient resource allocation, Pareto sub-optimal, welfare loss. Mohr §15.1
Pure Public Good 💡 Non-Rival & Non-Excludable A good or service displaying complete non-rivalry in consumption and complete non-excludability in access (e.g. national defense, flood warning systems, lighthouses, basic rule of law). Non-rival consumption, non-excludable access, zero marginal cost of extra user. Mohr §15.2
Non-Rivalry in Consumption Criterion 1 of Public Goods The property where one individual consuming or benefiting from a good does not in any way reduce the quantity, quality, or availability of that good for consumption by any other person. Undiminished availability, simultaneous consumption, MC = 0. Mohr §15.2
Non-Excludability in Access Criterion 2 of Public Goods The technological or economic impossibility (or prohibitive cost) of preventing non-paying consumers from accessing, enjoying, or benefiting from the good once it has been produced. Costly/impossible exclusion, open access, unpriced consumption. Mohr §15.2
Free-Rider Problem 💡 Incentive Failure A market failure stemming directly from non-excludability wherein rational self-interested consumers withhold payment hoping others will pay, causing private firms to underprovide or totally fail to supply the good. Consumption without payment, private market underprovision, necessitates tax funding. Mohr §15.2
Common Property Resources Rival & Non-Excludable Natural resources that are rival in consumption (depletable) but non-excludable in access (e.g. oceanic fisheries, shared communal grazing pasture), inexorably precipitating the Tragedy of the Commons. Rival but non-excludable, Tragedy of the Commons, resource depletion. Mohr §15.2
Negative Externality 💡 Spillover Cost (\(\text{MEC} > 0\)) An uncompensated cost or damage imposed by producers or consumers on third parties who are not participants in the market transaction (e.g. factory sulfur emissions polluting regional water sources). Uncompensated third-party cost, \(\text{MSC} > \text{MPC}\), market overproduction. Mohr §15.3
Marginal External Cost Symbol: \(\text{MEC}\) The additional monetary damage and unpriced social harm inflicted on innocent third parties for each additional unit of physical output produced. Damage per extra unit, vertical gap between MSC and MPC. Mohr §15.3
Marginal Social Cost Symbol: \(\text{MSC} = \text{MPC} + \text{MEC}\) The true, complete opportunity cost to society of producing an additional unit of output, defined as private internal production costs plus the marginal external cost: \(\text{MSC} = \text{MPC} + \text{MEC}\). Full social opportunity cost, \(\text{MPC} + \text{MEC}\). Mohr §15.3
Pigouvian Tax 💡 Symbol: \(t = \text{MEC}\) A specific per-unit corrective excise tax levied on polluters set equal to the Marginal External Cost at the socially optimum output level, forcing private firms to internalize the externality. Tax set equal to MEC, internalizes external cost, shifts MPC to MSC. Mohr §15.3
Deadweight Loss (DWL) 💡 Symbol: \(\text{DWL}\) The net loss of total economic welfare (combined consumer and producer surplus) caused by allocative distortion (such as uncorrected externalities or excise taxation) that is captured by no party. Permanent net surplus loss, allocative inefficiency, excess burden. Mohr §15.3
Progressive Tax 💡 Criteria: \(\text{MTR} > \text{ATR}\) A tax structure wherein the Average Tax Rate (\(\text{ATR} = \frac{\text{Tax}}{\text{Income}}\)) increases as personal taxable income increases, ensuring wealthier taxpayers pay a higher proportion of income (e.g. SA PIT: 18% to 45%). ATR rises with income, MTR > ATR, vertical equity. Mohr §15.4
Proportional Tax Criteria: \(\text{MTR} = \text{ATR}\) A flat tax structure in which the Average Tax Rate remains constant regardless of income level, meaning tax liability increases in direct linear proportion to income (e.g. SA Corporate Income Tax at 27%). Constant ATR across all incomes, MTR = ATR, flat percentage. Mohr §15.4
Regressive Tax Criteria: \(\text{MTR} < \text{ATR}\) A tax structure wherein the Average Tax Rate falls as income increases. While Value Added Tax (VAT at 15%) has a uniform statutory rate, it is economically regressive relative to income because poorer households consume a larger share of disposable earnings. ATR falls as income rises, MTR < ATR, disproportionate burden on poor. Mohr §15.4
Fiscal Drag (Bracket Creep) 💡 Inflation Tax Effect The automatic fiscal process whereby nominal wage increases push taxpayers into higher statutory income tax brackets during periods of inflation, elevating their real tax burden without any real increase in purchasing power. Nominal wage inflation, higher statutory tax bracket, unlegislated tax hike. Mohr §15.4
National Budget Deficit Condition: \(G > T\) The annual fiscal shortfall occurring when total government expenditure (\(G\)) exceeds total tax and non-tax revenues (\(T\)) during a given fiscal year, necessitating public debt issuance by the National Treasury. Spending exceeds revenue, Treasury bond borrowing, accumulates sovereign debt. Mohr §15.5

🧾 Direct vs Indirect Tax Incidence & The Elasticity Transmission Mechanism

Why statutory tax liability does NOT dictate who actually pays: how relative price elasticities of demand and supply determine the final economic burden between consumers and producers.

Step 1
Statutory Imposition on Producers (Indirect Tax)
Government legally levies a specific excise tax of \(t\) per unit (e.g. fuel levy or sin tax) on manufacturers/suppliers of a commodity. The legal obligation to remit revenue to SARS rests with the seller.
Step 2
Upward Vertical Supply Curve Shift
Because production costs increase by \(t\) per unit across all output volumes, the market supply curve shifts vertically upwards by exactly the monetary value of the tax: \(S_0 \rightarrow S_1 = S_0 + t\).
Step 3
Creation of the Tax Wedge
The consumer pays the higher market gross price (\(P_c\)), but the producer receives only the net price after deducting the tax remittance: \(P_p = P_c - t\). The vertical distance between \(P_c\) and \(P_p\) is the tax wedge.
Step 4
Economic Incidence Driven by Relative Elasticity
The distribution of the tax burden depends strictly on relative price elasticities:
  • Inelastic Demand (\(|E_d| < |E_s|\)): Consumers cannot easily substitute away; market price rises sharply (\(P_c \approx P_0 + t\)); buyers bear the overwhelming economic burden.
  • Elastic Demand (\(|E_d| > |E_s|\)): Consumers readily abandon the good; producers cannot pass the tax forward; sellers absorb the financial burden (\(P_p \ll P_0\)).
Step 5
Fiscal Revenue vs Deadweight Loss Triangle
Government collects tax revenue of \(T = t \times Q_1\) (represented by the tax rectangle). Because market output contracts from \(Q_0\) to \(Q_1\), mutually beneficial trades are destroyed, creating a Harberger deadweight loss (DWL) triangle of lost consumer and producer surplus.
Formal Tax Incidence Ratio: \(\frac{\text{Consumer Tax Burden}}{\text{Producer Tax Burden}} = \frac{\text{Price Elasticity of Supply } (E_s)}{\text{Price Elasticity of Demand } (|E_d|)}\)

🏛️ The Classical Crowding-Out Transmission Mechanism

The macroeconomic transmission chain through which deficit-financed public spending drives up interest rates and displaces productive private capital formation.

Step 1
Fiscal Stimulus / Budget Deficit Expansion
Government implements expansionary fiscal spending or confronts a structural tax shortfall, creating or widening the national budget deficit (\(G > T\)).
Step 2
Sovereign Treasury Bond Issuance
To finance the deficit without printing money, the National Treasury issues government bonds (long-term sovereign debt) in the domestic money and capital markets.
Step 3
Loanable Funds Competition & Interest Rate Surge
Heavy public sector borrowing expands the aggregate demand for loanable funds. In the domestic capital market, competition for investable private savings bids up nominal and real market interest rates (\(i \uparrow\)).
Step 4
Hurdle Rate Escalation for Private Capital
Private corporations face higher borrowing costs and higher bond yields. Marginal commercial investment projects whose expected internal rate of return falls below the elevated borrowing cost are canceled or shelved.
Step 5
Suppression of Private Investment (\(I \downarrow\))
Private investment in physical capital, equipment, and residential construction is suppressed (\(I \downarrow\)). The net expansion in Aggregate Demand is partially or fully neutralized, reducing long-run economic growth potential.
Deficit \(G > T\) ➔ Treasury Bonds \(\uparrow\) ➔ Loanable Funds Demand \(\uparrow\) ➔ Interest Rate \(i \uparrow\) ➔ Private Investment \(I \downarrow\) ➔ Crowding Out 💡

Economic Term & Symbol Official Exam Definition Key Keywords Emphasized Prescribed Citation
Inflation Symbol: \(\pi\) A continuous and considerable rise in the general price level across the entire economy over time, resulting in an irreversible decline in the purchasing power of money. Continuous process, considerable increase, general price level, purchasing power loss. Mohr §20.1
Consumer Price Index (CPI) 💡 Statistical Indicator A monthly statistical Laspeyres-type index that reflects the weighted average cost of a fixed representative basket of consumer goods and services purchased by typical urban households. Representative urban basket, weighted average index, includes consumer imports. Mohr §20.2
Headline Inflation Rate Symbol: \(\pi_{\text{headline}}\) The unadjusted twelve-month (year-on-year) percentage change in the total Consumer Price Index, including all volatile food, beverage, and energy components. Total all-items CPI change, includes volatile food & fuel, year-on-year %. Mohr §20.2
Core (Underlying) Inflation Symbol: \(\pi_{\text{core}}\) An adjusted inflation indicator derived by mathematically stripping highly volatile price categories (fresh agricultural food, vegetables, and energy) from the CPI to discern underlying trend inflation. Excludes volatile food & energy, underlying inflation trend, policy guidance. Mohr §20.2
GDP Price Deflator 💡 Implicit Price Index A comprehensive implicit price index capturing the prices of all final goods and services produced domestically, calculated as \(\frac{\text{Nominal GDP}}{\text{Real GDP}} \times 100\); includes capital goods but excludes imports. Domestically produced goods, includes capital goods, strictly excludes imports. Mohr §20.2
Demand-Pull Inflation 💡 Origin: Excess \(AD\) Inflation initiated by aggregate demand for goods and services outstripping aggregate productive supply at existing prices ("too much money chasing too few goods"), shifting the \(AD\) curve rightwards. Excess aggregate demand, AD shifts right, output and price level rise. Mohr §20.3
Cost-Push Inflation 💡 Origin: Supply Shock Inflation initiated by autonomous supply-side spikes in the costs of production (imported oil shocks, Rand exchange rate depreciation, wage hikes exceeding productivity), shifting the \(SAS\) curve leftwards. Rising production costs, SAS shifts left, supply-side shock. Mohr §20.3
Stagflation 💡 Condition: \(P \uparrow, Y \downarrow, u \uparrow\) The toxic economic dilemma of persistent high inflation occurring simultaneously with economic stagnation, falling real GDP output (\(Y \downarrow\)), and surging unemployment (\(u \uparrow\)). High inflation + high unemployment, adverse supply shock, falling real GDP. Mohr §20.3
Inflation Targeting SARB Band: 3% to 6% A monetary policy framework wherein the central bank commits publicly to using policy interest rate adjustments to anchor future headline CPI inflation within an explicit target range (3% to 6% in South Africa). Explicit numerical target (3%–6%), anchoring expectations, forward-looking repo adjustments. Mohr §20.4
Real Interest Rate Fisher Equation: \(r \approx i - \pi\) The nominal interest rate adjusted for inflation, representing the actual percentage growth in real purchasing power earned by lenders or paid by borrowers: \(r \approx i - \pi\). Inflation-adjusted return, Fisher Equation: \(r \approx i - \pi\), true purchasing power yield. Mohr §20.4
Redistribution Effects Arbitrary Wealth Shift Unanticipated inflation arbitrarily transfers real wealth from lenders (creditors) and fixed-income pensioners to borrowers (debtors), because loan contracts are repaid in depreciated currency units. Debtors gain, creditors lose, pensioners harmed, arbitrary wealth transfer. Mohr §20.4

🔥 The 5 Steps / Pillars of the Official Philip Mohr Inflation Definition

Why a single price increase is NOT inflation: the five non-negotiable structural criteria required by Philip Mohr (6th Edition) for an exam-grade inflation diagnosis.

Pillar 1
Dynamic, Continuous Process Over Time
Inflation is strictly an ongoing, sustained dynamic process. A once-off upward price adjustment (such as a single increase in the statutory VAT rate or a once-off municipal water tariff hike) does not constitute inflation unless it unleashes continuous price spirals.
Pillar 2
Considerable & Significant Magnitude
The overall price increase must be considerable and substantial in percentage magnitude. Trivial, negligible, or fractional fluctuations in price indices do not qualify as macroeconomic inflation.
Pillar 3
Broad-Based Across the General Price Level
The price rise must be pervasive across the general price level of the macroeconomy. A localized price increase in one or two isolated goods (e.g. avocado prices surging due to seasonal frost) is merely a relative price change, NOT inflation.
Pillar 4
Sustained Erosion of Purchasing Power
The continuous general price increase causes a direct, irreversible deterioration in the purchasing power of the national currency unit (\(1/P \downarrow\)), requiring economic agents to spend more money to obtain the identical real bundle of goods.
Pillar 5
Official Empirical Measurement via Index
The inflation rate must be statistically captured and verified through an officially recognized price index (such as the headline year-on-year percentage rate of change in the Consumer Price Index):
\(\pi_t = \frac{\text{CPI}_t - \text{CPI}_{t-1}}{\text{CPI}_{t-1}} \times 100\)

🌪️ The Wage-Price Spiral Cost-Push Propagation Mechanism

How an autonomous supply shock triggers competing income claims between trade unions and firms, mutating into persistent inflationary momentum.

Step 1
Autonomous External Supply Shock
The macroeconomy is hit by an exogenous supply-side shock (e.g. sharp depreciation of the Rand exchange rate, global crude oil price spike, or Eskom tariff escalation).
Step 2
SAS Leftward/Upward Shift & Price Surge
Imported input costs escalate, forcing the Short-Run Aggregate Supply curve upwards and leftwards (\(SAS_0 \rightarrow SAS_1\)), raising consumer price levels (\(P_0 \rightarrow P_1\)).
Step 3
Real Wage Deterioration & Union Demands
Higher living costs erode workers' real disposable wage (\(W/P \downarrow\)). Organized labor unions demand higher compensatory nominal wage increases to restore historic purchasing power.
Step 4
Business Cost Pass-Through
Employers concede to nominal wage increases exceeding productivity gains. To protect gross operating profit margins, businesses pass the higher wage bill onto retail selling prices.
Step 5
Self-Reinforcing Wage-Price Spiral
Higher consumer prices erode real wages once more, triggering a subsequent cycle of aggressive wage negotiations and cost increases. The inflation process becomes entrenched unless central bank interest rate policy firmly re-anchors expectations.
Cost Shock ➔ \(SAS\) Left ➔ Price Level \(P \uparrow\) ➔ Real Wage \(\downarrow\) ➔ Nominal Wage Demands \(\uparrow\) ➔ Firms Raise Prices \(P \uparrow\) ➔ Wage-Price Spiral

🌪️ AD-AS Macroeconomic Framework: Demand-Pull vs Cost-Push Stagflation

Mohr §20.3
Visual Derivation: Cost-Push Stagflation in the AD-AS Framework
Axis Labels Vertical: Aggregate Price Level (\(P\) / CPI)
Horizontal: Real Gross Domestic Product / National Output (\(Y\))
Valid Slope Range Aggregate Demand (\(AD\)): Downward-sloping (\(-15^\circ\) to \(-75^\circ\)) due to wealth, interest-rate, and international trade effects.
Short-Run Aggregate Supply (\(SAS\)): Upward-sloping (\(+15^\circ\) to \(+75^\circ\)) due to sticky nominal wages and temporary profit incentives.
Long-Run Aggregate Supply (\(LRAS\)): Strictly vertical (\(90^\circ\)) at full-employment potential output (\(Y_f\)).
Shift Triggers \(AD\) Shifts Right: Monetary easing (repo rate cut), fiscal expansion (\(G \uparrow\), \(T \downarrow\)), surge in consumer/business confidence, or export boom (\(X \uparrow\)).
\(SAS\) Shifts Left (Adverse Supply Shock): International crude oil price hikes, sharp Rand depreciation raising imported intermediate costs, nominal wage increases exceeding productivity, or Eskom electricity tariff increases.
\(LRAS\) Shifts Right: Capital deepening, technological innovation, workforce skills growth, or structural supply-side labor market reforms.
Impact on P & Y Demand-Pull Inflation (\(AD\) Right): Price level rises (\(P_0 \rightarrow P_1\)) and Real GDP expands above potential (\(Y_f \rightarrow Y_1\)), creating an inflationary output gap and reducing cyclical unemployment.
Cost-Push Stagflation (\(SAS\) Left): Price level rises (\(P_0 \rightarrow P_1\)) while Real GDP contracts below potential (\(Y_f \rightarrow Y_1\)), precipitating simultaneous high inflation and escalating unemployment — the policy dilemma where demand stimulus worsens inflation while tightening worsens unemployment.
