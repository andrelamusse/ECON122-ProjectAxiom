Money / Geld 💡 Symbol: \(M\) Any commodity or asset generally accepted by the public as payment for goods and services or in the legal discharge of debt, performing three non-negotiable functions: a medium of exchange, a unit of account, and a store of value. Medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value. Mohr §14.1
Fiduciary (Fiat) Money 💡 Symbol: Paper & Coin Money that is intrinsically worthless (paper notes, base metal coins, digital bits) but derives its economic value from public trust and the legal status conferred upon it by the state as legal tender. Intrinsically worthless, public trust, legal tender. Mohr §14.1
Narrow Money (M1) 💡 Symbol: \(M_1\) The primary transactional money aggregate comprising cash (notes and coins in active circulation outside the banking sector) plus all checkable and transmission (demand) deposits of the domestic private sector held with commercial banks. Cash outside banks, demand deposits, immediate transaction liquidity. Mohr §14.1
Quasi-Money (Near Money) 💡 Symbol: \(QM\) Short-term and medium-term deposits (including ordinary savings deposits) held by the private sector with commercial banks that serve as a store of value and can be converted into transaction money (\(M_1\)) rapidly at negligible cost. Short- & medium-term deposits, near-money liquidity, store of value. Mohr §14.1
Intermediate Money (M2) 💡 Symbol: \(M_2\) An intermediate monetary aggregate defined as narrow money (\(M_1\)) plus quasi-money: \(M_2 = M_1 + \text{Quasi-Money}\) (short-term and medium-term commercial bank deposits). \(M_1\) + Quasi-Money, intermediate liquidity, savings deposits. Mohr §14.1
Broad Money (M3) 💡 Symbol: \(M_3\) The most comprehensive official South African monetary measure, defined mathematically as \(M_2\) plus all long-term deposits (maturities exceeding 6 months) of the domestic private sector with monetary banking institutions. \(M_2\) + Long-term deposits, broadest aggregate, SARB anchor. Mohr §14.1
Credit (Deposit) Multiplier 💡 Symbol: \(m = 1/r\) The factor by which the commercial banking system can expand total bank deposits for every unit of new cash reserves injected, expressed as \(m = \frac{1}{r}\) (where \(r\) is the statutory cash reserve requirement ratio). Expand total deposits, \(m = 1/r\), statutory reserve ratio. Mohr §14.2
Cash Reserve Requirement 💡 Symbol: \(r\) The minimum percentage of total customer deposit liabilities that commercial banks are legally mandated to hold as non-interest-bearing cash reserves in an operational account at the SARB (currently 2.5% in South Africa). Statutory minimum %, non-interest-bearing SARB vault reserve, credit governor. Mohr §14.2
Repurchase (Repo) Rate 💡 Symbol: \(i_{\text{repo}}\) The benchmark policy interest rate at which the South African Reserve Bank provides cash liquidity to commercial banks at weekly refinancing auctions to fund their daily liquidity shortage. SARB benchmark policy rate, weekly repo auctions, liquidity deficit. Mohr §14.4
Prime Overdraft Rate 💡 Symbol: \(i_{\text{prime}}\) The benchmark commercial lending rate charged by private banks to their most creditworthy corporate and private clients, established in South Africa by convention at Repo Rate + 3.50%. Commercial bank benchmark rate, fixed spread over repo (+3.50%). Mohr §14.4
Active Cash Balances (L1) 💡 Symbol: \(L_1(Y)\) Money demanded and held for the transactions motive and precautionary motive; determined directly by and a positive function of national income (\(Y\)): \(L_1 = f(Y)\), completely independent of the interest rate. Transactions & precautionary motives, positive function of income \(Y\), interest-inelastic. Mohr §14.3
Speculative Balances (L2) 💡 Symbol: \(L_2(i)\) Money held as a liquid asset to take advantage of expected movements in bond prices and interest rates; an inverse (negative) function of the market interest rate (\(i\)): \(L_2 = f(i)\). Speculative asset demand, inverse function of interest rate \(i\), opportunity cost of cash. Mohr §14.3
Liquidity Preference 💡 Symbol: \(M_d = L_1(Y) + L_2(i)\) Keynes's theory that individuals hold money for three distinct motives: transactions motive (\(L_1\)), precautionary motive (\(L_1\)), and speculative motive (\(L_2\)). Active balances \(L_1\) depend positively on income (\(Y\)), while passive balances \(L_2\) are inversely related to the interest rate (\(i\)). Transactions & precautionary (\(L_1\)), speculative (\(L_2\)), \(f(Y)\) and \(f(i)\). Mohr §14.3
Liquidity Trap 💡 Symbol: Horizontal \(M_d\) A macroeconomic situation where nominal interest rates are so low that the speculative demand for money becomes perfectly elastic (horizontal). Further injections of money supply are hoarded as cash, rendering expansionary monetary policy ineffective at lowering interest rates. Infinitely elastic speculative demand, horizontal \(M_d\) segment, monetary policy impotence. Mohr §14.3

🏦 The Commercial Bank Credit Creation & Deposit Expansion Process

How private commercial banks expand the money supply through fractional reserve banking, and the real-world leakages that constrain the theoretical multiplier.

Step 1
Primary Cash Inflow to Initial Bank
An economic agent deposits a primary injection of \(\Delta R = \text{R}10\,000\) in new legal tender cash into Commercial Bank A, increasing bank assets (cash reserves) and liabilities (checkable deposit) equally.
Step 2
Statutory Cash Reserve Retention: Cash Reserve Requirement 💡
Assuming a statutory reserve requirement of \(r = 10\%\) (\(0.10\)), Bank A is legally required to hold \(\text{R}1\,000\) in reserve with the SARB. This leaves \(\text{R}9\,000\) as excess loanable reserves.
Step 3
Credit Extension & New Deposit Creation
Bank A lends the \(\text{R}9\,000\) excess reserves to Borrower 1 by crediting a demand deposit. Bank A has literally created \(\text{R}9\,000\) in new book money out of thin air. Borrower 1 writes a check/EFT to pay a supplier.
Step 4
Secondary Interbank Expansion Rounds
The supplier deposits the \(\text{R}9\,000\) into Commercial Bank B. Bank B must retain \(10\%\) (\(\text{R}900\)) and lends out the remaining \(90\%\) (\(\text{R}8\,100\)) to Borrower 2, propagating across banks in a diminishing geometric sequence: \(\text{R}10\,000 + \text{R}9\,000 + \text{R}8\,100 + \text{R}7\,290 + \dots\)
Step 5
Theoretical Maximum Cumulative Money Creation: Credit Multiplier 💡
Summing the infinite convergent series produces the maximum total deposit expansion:
\(\Delta D = \Delta R \times \frac{1}{r} = \text{R}10\,000 \times \frac{1}{0.10} = \text{R}10\,000 \times 10 = \text{R}100\,000\)
Net newly created credit money equals \(\Delta D - \Delta R = \text{R}100\,000 - \text{R}10\,000 = \text{R}90\,000\).
Step 6
Real-World Constraints & Cash Drains
In practice, the multiplier is significantly smaller than \(\frac{1}{r}\) due to: (a) Cash drain (the public retains currency rather than re-depositing), (b) Excess reserves (risk-averse banks hold precautionary liquidity), and (c) Lack of creditworthy borrower demand.

📈 The SARB Repurchase (Repo) Rate Monetary Policy Transmission Mechanism

The chronological six-stage transmission mechanism through which central bank interest rate decisions propagate through the commercial financial system to manage aggregate demand and anchor inflation expectations.

Step 1
SARB MPC Policy Rate Decision: Repo Rate 💡
To counteract rising demand-pull or wage-push inflation expectations, the SARB Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) raises the benchmark Repurchase (Repo) Rate 💡 (\(i_{\text{repo}} \uparrow\)) at its weekly Wednesday liquidity refinancing auction.
Step 2
Interbank & Prime Overdraft Pass-Through: Prime Rate 💡
Commercial banks face an immediate increase in the cost of refinancing their daily liquidity deficit at the SARB window. Within 24 hours, commercial banks pass on this cost by lifting the benchmark Prime Overdraft Rate 💡 (\(i_{\text{prime}} \uparrow = i_{\text{repo}} + 3.50\%\)) and floating deposit rates.
Step 3
Credit Channel Tightening & Cost Escalation
Borrowing costs for households and corporations escalate across all credit agreements (mortgage bonds, vehicle asset finance, business overdrafts). Concurrently, the incentive to save rises as fixed-deposit yields increase.
Step 4
Contraction in Private Domestic Expenditure
Higher debt servicing costs erode household disposable income, suppressing autonomous consumption of durables (\(C \downarrow\)). Simultaneously, higher financing hurdle rates lead business firms to postpone capital expenditure (\(I \downarrow\)).
Step 5
Aggregate Demand Contraction
Reduced domestic absorption contracts total expenditure, shifting the Aggregate Demand curve downwards/leftwards (\(AD = C + I + G + X - Z \downarrow\)), eliminating the positive output gap.
Step 6
Disinflation & Price Stability: Headline Inflation 💡
Firms face excess inventory and dampening demand, reducing their pricing power. Upward pressure on consumer prices dissipates, driving headline CPI inflation 💡 (\(\pi \downarrow\)) back toward the SARB 4.5% target midpoint.
Repo Rate \(i_{\text{repo}} \uparrow\) 💡 ➔ Prime Rate \(i_{\text{prime}} \uparrow\) 💡 ➔ Credit Cost \(\uparrow\) ➔ Private Spending \(C, I \downarrow\) ➔ Aggregate Demand \(AD \downarrow\) ➔ Inflation Rate \(\pi \downarrow\) 💡

Market Failure 💡 Allocative Inefficiency A failure of the unregulated free market mechanism to achieve allocative efficiency, occurring when Marginal Social Benefit does not equal Marginal Social Cost (\(\text{MSB} \ne \text{MSC}\)), resulting in social overproduction or underprovision of commodities. Allocative efficiency, \(\text{MSB} \ne \text{MSC}\), over-/underprovision. Mohr §15.1
Pure Public Good 💡 Non-Rival & Non-Excludable A good characterized by both non-rivalry in consumption (one person's use does not diminish another's) and non-excludability in access (it is impossible or prohibitively costly to exclude non-payers from enjoying it). Non-rivalry, non-excludability, zero marginal cost. Mohr §15.2
Non-Rivalry in Consumption 💡 Criterion 1 of Public Goods A condition where one person's consumption of a good does not reduce the quantity or quality available for consumption by others, meaning the marginal cost of admitting an additional consumer is strictly zero. Undiminished availability, simultaneous consumption, MC = 0. Mohr §15.2
Non-Excludability in Access 💡 Criterion 2 of Public Goods A property where it is technically impossible or prohibitively expensive to prevent non-payers from accessing and benefiting from a good or service once it has been provided. Costly/impossible exclusion, open access, unpriced consumption. Mohr §15.2
Free-Rider Problem 💡 Incentive Failure A market incentive failure where rational individuals consume a non-excludable good without contributing to its cost, causing private competitive markets to underprovide or completely fail to supply the socially desirable good. Consumption without payment, private market underprovision, necessitates tax funding. Mohr §15.2
Common Property Resources 💡 Rival & Non-Excludable Environmental or natural resources that are rival in consumption but non-excludable in access (e.g. oceanic fish stocks or communal pastures), inevitably precipitating overexploitation (the Tragedy of the Commons). Rival but non-excludable, Tragedy of the Commons, resource depletion. Mohr §15.2
Negative Production Externality 💡 Spillover Cost (\(\text{MEC} > 0\)) An uncompensated cost imposed on third parties during production (such as environmental pollution), causing Marginal Social Cost to exceed Marginal Private Cost (\(\text{MSC} > \text{MPC}\)), leading the unregulated market to overproduce relative to the social optimum. Uncompensated third-party cost, \(\text{MSC} > \text{MPC}\), market overproduction. Mohr §15.3
Marginal External Cost 💡 Symbol: \(\text{MEC}\) The uncompensated external cost or environmental damage inflicted on third parties by producing one additional unit of output: \(\text{MEC} = \text{MSC} - \text{MPC}\). Damage per extra unit, vertical gap between MSC and MPC. Mohr §15.3
Marginal Social Cost 💡 Symbol: \(\text{MSC} = \text{MPC} + \text{MEC}\) The total cost to society of producing one extra unit of output, defined mathematically as Marginal Private Cost plus Marginal External Cost: \(\text{MSC} = \text{MPC} + \text{MEC}\). Full social opportunity cost, \(\text{MPC} + \text{MEC}\). Mohr §15.3
Pigouvian Tax 💡 Symbol: \(t = \text{MEC}\) A specific per-unit corrective tax set exactly equal to the Marginal External Cost (\(t = \text{MEC}\)) levied on polluters to internalize the externality, shifting \(\text{MPC}\) up to \(\text{MSC}\) to restore the social optimum. Tax set equal to MEC, internalizes external cost, shifts MPC to MSC. Mohr §15.3
Deadweight Loss (DWL) 💡 Symbol: \(\text{DWL}\) The permanent loss of combined consumer and producer surplus caused by market distortions, allocative inefficiency, or taxes that is not recovered by any economic agent or the state. Permanent net surplus loss, allocative distortion, excess burden. Mohr §15.3
Progressive Tax 💡 Criteria: \(\text{MTR} > \text{ATR}\) A tax structure where the Average Tax Rate increases as taxable income rises (\(\text{MTR} > \text{ATR}\)), ensuring that higher-income earners pay a proportionally larger percentage of their income in tax (e.g. South African personal income tax). ATR rises with income, MTR > ATR, vertical equity. Mohr §15.4
Proportional Tax 💡 Criteria: \(\text{MTR} = \text{ATR}\) A tax system where the Average Tax Rate remains constant across all income levels (\(\text{MTR} = \text{ATR}\)), so that all income earners pay the exact same percentage of their taxable income (e.g. SA Corporate Income Tax at 27%). Constant ATR across all incomes, MTR = ATR, flat percentage. Mohr §15.4
Regressive Tax 💡 Criteria: \(\text{MTR} < \text{ATR}\) A tax system where the Average Tax Rate falls as taxable income rises (\(\text{MTR} < \text{ATR}\)), placing a proportionally heavier financial burden on low-income earners (e.g. South African VAT at 15% or specific excise duties). ATR falls as income rises, MTR < ATR, burden on poor. Mohr §15.4
Fiscal Drag (Bracket Creep) 💡 Inflation Tax Effect The automatic phenomenon where inflation pushes nominal wages into higher statutory tax brackets without an increase in real purchasing power, thereby increasing the taxpayer's average tax rate without any statutory rate increase. Nominal wage inflation, higher statutory tax bracket, unlegislated tax hike. Mohr §15.4
National Budget Deficit 💡 Condition: \(G > T\) A fiscal condition where total government expenditures exceed total government tax revenues (\(G > T\)) in a single fiscal year, requiring the Treasury to finance the gap through public borrowing and bond issuance. Spending exceeds revenue, Treasury bond borrowing, sovereign debt. Mohr §15.5
Crowding-Out Effect 💡 Mechanism: \(G \uparrow \rightarrow i \uparrow \rightarrow I \downarrow\) The reduction in private consumption and investment spending that occurs when large government budget deficits and increased state borrowing bid up domestic market interest rates. Displaces private investment, deficit financing bids up \(i\), neutralizes stimulus. Mohr §15.5

🧾 Direct vs Indirect Tax Incidence & The Elasticity Transmission Mechanism

Why statutory tax liability does NOT dictate who actually pays: how relative price elasticities of demand and supply determine the final economic burden between consumers and producers.

Step 1
Statutory Imposition on Producers (Indirect Tax)
Government legally levies a specific excise tax of \(t\) per unit (e.g. fuel levy or sin tax) on manufacturers/suppliers of a commodity. The legal obligation to remit revenue to SARS rests with the seller.
Step 2
Upward Vertical Supply Curve Shift
Because production costs increase by \(t\) per unit across all output volumes, the market supply curve shifts vertically upwards by exactly the monetary value of the tax: \(S_0 \rightarrow S_1 = S_0 + t\).
Step 3
Creation of the Tax Wedge
The consumer pays the higher market gross price (\(P_c\)), but the producer receives only the net price after deducting the tax remittance: \(P_p = P_c - t\). The vertical distance between \(P_c\) and \(P_p\) is the tax wedge.
Step 4
Economic Incidence Driven by Relative Elasticity
The distribution of the tax burden depends strictly on relative price elasticities:
  • Inelastic Demand (\(|E_d| < |E_s|\)): Consumers cannot easily substitute away; market price rises sharply (\(P_c \approx P_0 + t\)); buyers bear the overwhelming economic burden.
  • Elastic Demand (\(|E_d| > |E_s|\)): Consumers readily abandon the good; producers cannot pass the tax forward; sellers absorb the financial burden (\(P_p \ll P_0\)).
Step 5
Fiscal Revenue vs Deadweight Loss Triangle: Deadweight Loss 💡
Government collects tax revenue of \(T = t \times Q_1\) (represented by the tax rectangle). Because market output contracts from \(Q_0\) to \(Q_1\), mutually beneficial trades are destroyed, creating a Harberger deadweight loss (DWL) 💡 triangle of lost consumer and producer surplus.
Formal Tax Incidence Ratio: \(\frac{\text{Consumer Tax Burden}}{\text{Producer Tax Burden}} = \frac{\text{Price Elasticity of Supply } (E_s)}{\text{Price Elasticity of Demand } (|E_d|)}\)

🏛️ The Classical Crowding-Out Transmission Mechanism

The macroeconomic transmission chain through which deficit-financed public spending drives up interest rates and displaces productive private capital formation.

Step 1
Fiscal Stimulus / Budget Deficit Expansion: Budget Deficit 💡
Government implements expansionary fiscal spending or confronts a structural tax shortfall, creating or widening the national budget deficit 💡 (\(G > T\)).
Step 2
Sovereign Treasury Bond Issuance
To finance the deficit without printing money, the National Treasury issues government bonds (long-term sovereign debt) in the domestic money and capital markets.
Step 3
Loanable Funds Competition & Interest Rate Surge
Heavy public sector borrowing expands the aggregate demand for loanable funds. In the domestic capital market, competition for investable private savings bids up nominal and real market interest rates (\(i \uparrow\)).
Step 4
Hurdle Rate Escalation for Private Capital
Private corporations face higher borrowing costs and higher bond yields. Marginal commercial investment projects whose expected internal rate of return falls below the elevated borrowing cost are canceled or shelved.
Step 5
Suppression of Private Investment (\(I \downarrow\)): Crowding Out 💡
Private investment in physical capital, equipment, and residential construction is suppressed (\(I \downarrow\)). The net expansion in Aggregate Demand is partially or fully neutralized, reducing long-run economic growth potential.
Deficit \(G > T\) 💡 ➔ Treasury Bonds \(\uparrow\) ➔ Loanable Funds Demand \(\uparrow\) ➔ Interest Rate \(i \uparrow\) ➔ Private Investment \(I \downarrow\) ➔ Crowding Out 💡

Inflation / Inflasie 💡 Symbol: \(\pi\) A continuous and considerable increase in the general price level of goods and services across the entire economy over time, resulting in an ongoing drop in the purchasing power of money. Continuous process, considerable increase, general price level, purchasing power loss. Mohr §20.1
Consumer Price Index (CPI) 💡 Statistical Indicator A statistical price index reflecting the weighted average cost of a fixed representative basket of consumer goods and services purchased by typical urban households, utilized as the official benchmark for headline inflation. Demand-Pull Inflation 💡 Origin: Excess \(AD\) Inflation initiated when aggregate demand expands faster than aggregate supply (\(AD > AS\)) at or near full employment ('too much money chasing too few goods'), shifting the Aggregate Demand (\(AD\)) curve rightward. Excess aggregate demand, AD shifts right, output and price level rise. Mohr §20.3

Real Interest Rate 💡 Fisher: \(r \approx i - \pi\) The nominal interest rate adjusted for inflation according to the Fisher Equation: \(r \approx i - \pi\), measuring the actual percentage growth in purchasing power earned by lenders or paid by borrowers. Inflation-adjusted return, Fisher Equation: \(r \approx i - \pi\), true purchasing power yield. Mohr §20.4
Redistribution Effects 💡 Arbitrary Wealth Shift The arbitrary transfer of real wealth caused by unanticipated inflation: debtors (borrowers) gain while creditors (lenders) and fixed-income pensioners lose, because debt liabilities are repaid in depreciated currency units. Debtors gain, creditors lose, pensioners harmed, arbitrary wealth transfer. Mohr §20.4

🔥 The 5 Steps / Pillars of the Official Philip Mohr Inflation 💡 Definition

Why a single price increase is NOT inflation: the five non-negotiable structural criteria required by Philip Mohr (6th Edition) for an exam-grade inflation diagnosis.

Pillar 1
Dynamic, Continuous Process Over Time
Inflation is strictly an ongoing, sustained dynamic process. A once-off upward price adjustment (such as a single increase in the statutory VAT rate or a once-off municipal water tariff hike) does not constitute inflation unless it unleashes continuous price spirals.
Pillar 2
Considerable & Significant Magnitude
The overall price increase must be considerable and substantial in percentage magnitude. Trivial, negligible, or fractional fluctuations in price indices do not qualify as macroeconomic inflation.
Pillar 3
Broad-Based Across the General Price Level
The price rise must be pervasive across the general price level of the macroeconomy. A localized price increase in one or two isolated goods (e.g. avocado prices surging due to seasonal frost) is merely a relative price change, NOT inflation.
Pillar 4
Sustained Erosion of Purchasing Power
The continuous general price increase causes a direct, irreversible deterioration in the purchasing power of the national currency unit (\(1/P \downarrow\)), requiring economic agents to spend more money to obtain the identical real bundle of goods.
Pillar 5
Official Empirical Measurement via Index
The inflation rate must be statistically captured and verified through an officially recognized price index (such as the headline year-on-year percentage rate of change in the Consumer Price Index 💡):
\(\pi_t = \frac{\text{CPI}_t - \text{CPI}_{t-1}}{\text{CPI}_{t-1}} \times 100\)

🌪️ The Wage-Price Spiral Cost-Push 💡 Propagation Mechanism

How an autonomous supply shock triggers competing income claims between trade unions and firms, mutating into persistent inflationary momentum.

Step 1
Autonomous External Supply Shock
The macroeconomy is hit by an exogenous supply-side shock (e.g. sharp depreciation of the Rand exchange rate, global crude oil price spike, or Eskom tariff escalation).
Step 2
SAS Leftward/Upward Shift & Price Surge
Imported input costs escalate, forcing the Short-Run Aggregate Supply curve upwards and leftwards (\(SAS_0 \rightarrow SAS_1\)), raising consumer price levels (\(P_0 \rightarrow P_1\)).
Step 3
Real Wage Deterioration & Union Demands
Higher living costs erode workers' real disposable wage (\(W/P \downarrow\)). Organized labor unions demand higher compensatory nominal wage increases to restore historic purchasing power.
Step 4
Business Cost Pass-Through
Employers concede to nominal wage increases exceeding productivity gains. To protect gross operating profit margins, businesses pass the higher wage bill onto retail selling prices.
Step 5
Self-Reinforcing Wage-Price Spiral: Cost-Push Spiral 💡
Higher consumer prices erode real wages once more, triggering a subsequent cycle of aggressive wage negotiations and cost increases. The inflation 💡 process becomes entrenched unless central bank interest rate policy firmly re-anchors expectations.
Cost Shock 💡 ➔ \(SAS\) Left ➔ Price Level \(P \uparrow\) ➔ Real Wage \(\downarrow\) ➔ Nominal Wage Demands \(\uparrow\) ➔ Firms Raise Prices \(P \uparrow\) ➔ Stagflation Risk 💡

Impact on P & Y Unregulated Market Equilibrium (\(E_1\)): Market overproduces (\(Q_1 > Q_2\)) at an artificially low price (\(P_1 < P_2\)), generating net Deadweight Loss (DWL) 💡 from excessive pollution.
Corrected Social Optimum (\(E_2\)): Output contracts to optimal volume (\(Q_2 < Q_1\)) and consumer price rises to reflect full social opportunity cost (\(P_2 > P_1\)), completely eradicating Deadweight Loss 💡 and restoring Pareto efficiency.

Visual Derivation: Cost-Push Stagflation in the AD-AS Framework
Axis Labels Vertical: Aggregate Price Level (\(P\) / CPI)
Horizontal: Real Gross Domestic Product / National Output (\(Y\))
Valid Slope Range Aggregate Demand (\(AD\)): Downward-sloping (\(-15^\circ\) to \(-75^\circ\)) due to wealth, interest-rate, and international trade effects.
Short-Run Aggregate Supply (\(SAS\)): Upward-sloping (\(+15^\circ\) to \(+75^\circ\)) due to sticky nominal wages and temporary profit incentives.
Long-Run Aggregate Supply (\(LRAS\)): Strictly vertical (\(90^\circ\)) at full-employment potential output (\(Y_f\)).
Shift Triggers \(AD\) Shifts Right: Monetary easing (repo rate cut 💡), fiscal expansion (\(G \uparrow\), \(T \downarrow\)), surge in consumer/business confidence, or export boom (\(X \uparrow\)).
\(SAS\) Shifts Left (Adverse Supply Shock): International crude oil price hikes, sharp Rand depreciation raising imported intermediate costs, nominal wage increases exceeding productivity, or Eskom electricity tariff increases.
\(LRAS\) Shifts Right: Capital deepening, technological innovation, workforce skills growth, or structural supply-side labor market reforms.
Impact on P & Y Demand-Pull Inflation (\(AD\) Right) 💡: Price level rises (\(P_0 \rightarrow P_1\)) and Real GDP expands above potential (\(Y_f \rightarrow Y_1\)), creating an inflationary output gap and reducing cyclical unemployment.
Cost-Push Stagflation (\(SAS\) Left) 💡: Price level rises (\(P_0 \rightarrow P_1\)) while Real GDP contracts below potential (\(Y_f \rightarrow Y_1\)), precipitating simultaneous high inflation and escalating unemployment — the policy dilemma where demand stimulus worsens inflation while tightening worsens unemployment.
